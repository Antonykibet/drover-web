What you see

Three numbers that never meet

The fee is in the contract. The hours are in a spreadsheet somebody chases on Friday. What the work cost to deliver is nowhere at all, so the one subtraction that matters never gets made.

Drover puts all three on one row, for every phase, while you can still do something about it.

Fee
What the client agreed for this phase. Taken off the contract once, never retyped.
Cost
What the people on it cost the firm an hour, times the hours the clock recorded. Not a charge-out rate and no client ever sees it. You never type it either — it follows whoever holds the task, so moving work to someone senior recosts the phase by itself.
Profit
The fee less the cost, once the phase is finished. The number that usually exists nowhere, and the reason for the product.

What you find first

The same studio, from 65% to minus 12%

Every phase here was sold by the same people, to the same standard, in the same year. One kept 65 cents of every dollar. One handed back twelve. Nobody in the studio could have told you which was which.

That spread is not a failure of effort. It is what happens when a fixed fee meets a job nobody measured — and it is the first thing the product shows you.

How it works

No timesheets. Not one.

Hours come from a task's own status changes — the gap between going In progress and leaving again, clipped to working hours. Nobody rounds up on a Friday. Nobody rebuilds a fortnight from memory.

Which bucket an hour lands in is derived too, from what the task is attached to. Nobody picks it from a dropdown, so nobody can talk the number up in a review.

Team Schedule

The STATE column at the end of every row is the timesheet. Moving a task into In progress starts the clock and moving it out stops it, which is where 3h of 3h on the RFI came from. The BUCKET column beside it is derived the same way — nobody chose it.

The quiet number

Put a figure on the work you gave away

Every studio absorbs work. The reselection after the client saw it in the room, the delivery nobody scheduled into a fee, the supplier chased for the third time. None of it is worth the argument on its own, which is exactly why it never gets counted.

For whoever runs the week

Run the week from the same records

A principal wants to know whether the job made money. Whoever runs the studio wants to know who is drowning and what is stuck with a client. Both come out of the same records, so there is no second system to keep and no week where the two disagree.

You can see who is carrying the week and who has room, without asking anyone — the estimate on every task was set when the phase was planned and has been spreading across their calendar since.
